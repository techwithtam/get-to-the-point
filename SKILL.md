# Get to the point

Put the request first and the detail behind it. Keep the detail ready for questions.

## The opening

Open with three short sentences:

1. What is needed.
2. Why it matters.
3. What happens without action.

For example: "Please approve Northline by Friday. It is the only option that passed both pilots within budget. Without approval, we renew the current contract at $18,000."

The reader may need nothing more.

## Steps

1. **Identify the reader.** Name who will read this and what they must do. Ask the user only for what the draft does not show.
2. **Set one purpose.** The message informs, explores options, or requests a decision. Split a draft that does more than one.
3. **Write the opening.** Use the three sentences above. For an update, state what happened, why it matters, and what happens next.
4. **Answer direct questions first.** Start with yes or no. State the conditions after the answer.
5. **Move the detail.** Keep what the reader needs to act. Move the reasoning below the opening. Hold the rest for likely questions.
6. **Prepare answers.** List the questions this reader is likely to ask, with short answers from the draft. Tell the user when the draft has no answer.
7. **Edit the language.** Replace terms the reader does not use. Split any sentence over about twelve words that carries two ideas.
8. **Choose the format.** Use writing to inform and a meeting to explore options. For a decision, send the background first and use the meeting to decide. An urgent message must make sense without a follow-up.
9. **State the next step.** Name the action, the owner, and the date.

## Rules

- Keep the user's facts and voice. Mark anything you inferred.
- Ask for a missing date, number, or owner.
- Keep real uncertainty. State it after the answer.
- When a reader questions one detail, answer it. Change the opening only if the answer changes the decision.
- After a decision, offer a short written record of the decision, next steps, and owners.

## Output

1. **The message.**
2. **Likely questions.** Each with a short answer.
3. **What changed.** One line for each item you moved, split, or cut.
4. **Questions for you.** Anything the draft is missing.

## Check before you finish

- The reader could act on the first three sentences alone.
- Every fact from the draft has a place or appears in What changed.
- The message names who does what next.

## Examples

| Not this | Do this | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| If the remaining risks stay low and vendor access comes through by Friday, we should be able to deliver by March 1. | Yes, we can deliver by March 1 if vendor access is confirmed by Friday. | The answer comes first. The condition follows. |
| Background, then analysis, then a recommendation on page four. | The recommendation in the first sentence, with the analysis below it. | The reader can judge the reasoning once they know the recommendation. |
| We need to align on the go-forward approach for the migration. | Please approve the move to Northline by Friday. | Name the action, the subject, and the date. |
| Someone questioned a number, so every report now includes the full data appendix. | The report stays the same, and the data is ready if someone asks. | One question does not change what most readers need. |
| Thanks, everyone. Great discussion. | We approved Northline. Priya signs the contract by Friday. | The written record states the decision and the owner. |
