# Get to the point

Rewrite the user's draft so a senior reader can understand the request and act on it from the opening alone. The detail in the draft still matters, because it supports the recommendation and prepares the user for questions, but it does not need to lead the message.

People who have done the detailed work tend to report all of it, in the order they learned it. Their reader usually has limited time and wants to know what is being asked of them. Reverse that order: state the outcome first, then offer the reasoning that supports it.

## The opening

Open every message with three specific sentences that state what is needed, why it matters, and what happens if the reader does not act. These sentences may be all the reader requires. When the reader wants more, the reasoning follows below, and they can assess it because they already know the recommendation.

For example: "Please approve Northline as our scheduling system by Friday, March 6. It is the only option that passed both front-desk pilots within budget. Without approval by Friday, we will miss the April migration window and the current contract will renew for another year at $18,000."

## Steps

1. **Identify the audience.** Determine who will read the message, what they are responsible for, how much detail they usually expect, and whether they will relay it to others. Use the draft and the user's description first, and ask only for what is missing. A sponsor who briefs senior leadership needs wording they can repeat, so write for that second audience as well.
2. **Define the purpose.** Decide whether the message informs, explores options, or requests a decision. Each purpose needs different information, so separate them into different messages or sections when the draft combines them, and tell the user what you separated.
3. **Write the opening.** Draft the three sentences from the user's facts. In a status update with no request, state what happened, why it matters, and what happens next.
4. **Answer direct questions first.** When the reader has asked a direct question, start with the answer and state the conditions directly after it. A long qualified answer to a yes-or-no question reads as evasion, even when every qualification is accurate. Keep any real uncertainty in the message.
5. **Organize the supporting detail.** Keep in the message only the facts the reader needs in order to act. Move the reasoning below the opening or into an attachment, and hold the remaining detail for the prepared answers.
6. **Prepare answers to likely questions.** List the questions this reader is most likely to raise, with concise answers drawn from the draft. Readers often test one detail to confirm that the author knows the work, and a specific answer builds confidence in the rest of the message. If the draft contains no answer, tell the user.
7. **Edit for the reader.** Replace jargon, acronyms, and field-specific terms the reader would not use, and keep the technical terms they do use. Split a sentence only when it carries more than one idea. Keep whole, professional sentences, along with any condition or causal link the reader needs.
8. **Choose the format.** Written communication suits information, and a working session suits exploring options. For a decision, send the background before the meeting and use the meeting to deliberate. An urgent message must state what happened, why it matters, and what happens without action, because the reader may need to respond without a follow-up.
9. **State the next step.** End with the specific action, its owner, and its due date. When a reply leaves the next step unclear, draft a follow-up that asks for it directly.

## Rules

- Preserve the user's facts and voice, and mark anything you inferred.
- Ask the user for any missing date, figure, or owner.
- When a reader questions one detail, answer it, and change the opening only if the answer changes the decision. One question does not mean every future version needs more detail.
- After a decision, offer to draft a short written record of the decision, next steps, and owners for the people affected.

## Output

Return four parts in this order:

1. **The message.** The rewritten draft, ready to send.
2. **Likely questions.** Each question with a concise answer.
3. **What changed.** One line for each item you moved, split, or cut, so the user can restore it.
4. **Questions for you.** Anything missing from the draft that the user needs to supply.

## Completion check

Before returning the result, confirm that the reader could act on the opening alone, that every fact from the draft appears in the message, in an answer, or under What changed, and that the message names who does what next.

## Examples

| Not this | Do this | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| If the remaining risks stay low and vendor access comes through by Friday, we should be able to deliver by March 1. | Yes, we can deliver by March 1 if vendor access is confirmed by Friday. | The reader asked a direct question, so the answer comes first and the condition follows it. |
| Background, then analysis, then a recommendation on page four. | The recommendation in the first sentence, with the analysis below it. | The reader can assess the reasoning once they know the recommendation. |
| We need to align on the go-forward approach for the migration. | Please approve the move to Northline by Friday. | The request names the action, the subject, and the date. |
| Someone questioned a number, so every report now includes the full data appendix. | The report stays the same, and the data is ready if someone asks. | One question does not change what most readers need. |
| Thanks, everyone. Great discussion. | We approved Northline, and Priya will sign the contract by Friday. | A written record states the decision and its owner, so people work from the same version. |
